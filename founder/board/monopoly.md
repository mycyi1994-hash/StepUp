# Monopoly lens
Score: 3 | Vote: PASS

## What is strong
- **There is a small market to own.** "Korean running crews" is narrow and specific enough to dominate before expanding. The product is built for the crew, not the lone runner: crew rooms, crew chat, crew levels, flash runs, and a shared daily crew stamina pool.
- **A network effect could form here.** The shared pool, the same-crew headcount bonus and crew levels all make the app more valuable as more of your crew joins. Leaving costs you your crew's progress. Crew lock-in is the only moat this lens can see.
- **The thesis is honest about the usual failure.** Capped earnings, points with no cash value and "app first, token second" avoid the emissions-driven collapse the founder describes. That makes the business possible, but it does not make it a monopoly.
- **Building costs little.** One founder with AI tools has shipped a lot of features.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **There is no distribution plan.** How the first 100 users arrive and what each one costs are both unanswered. The app is not on Google Play and has no user metrics. It is also Android-only, and a crew app that leaves out some members of a crew breaks the network effect it depends on. A product with no way to reach buyers is not a business.
2. **Nothing protects it from copycats, and nothing is 10x better.** GPS tracking, leaderboards, crew chat and gamified points are each an incremental feature, not a breakthrough. The founder's own document admits the moat-shaped mechanic, the headcount bonus, is "effectively 0 today" because nobody has verified a phone number. A larger running or community app could copy the crew-pool mechanic in month six. Who already serves Korean runners and crews is not stated in the brief, and that is the first thing this lens would need to know.
3. **There is no revenue and no secret.** Without a revenue model, a monopoly has no profit to protect. The strongest secret on offer ("build the app before the token") is a lesson from other people's failures that anyone can read. It is not proprietary knowledge. On top of this, legal review is still pending for points, shoe trading, the wallet and the location-service filing. That may also limit how far the game layer, the stickiest part, can go.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Name the specific crews (how many, how big, where) that you could get running on StepUp in the first 30 days. How do they hear about it, and what does each one cost?
- Who else is within reach of a Korean crew leader today, and why would that leader move a whole crew's habits to you?
- What is the secret? What do you know about Korean crew running that incumbents get wrong?
- Why now? What changed in 2026 that makes this possible or needed?
- In ten years, what is the source of durability: the crew graph, a brand, or data? What are you doing today to compound it?
- Is there an iOS plan? What share of a typical crew can actually install this?
- What will it charge, and who pays: runners, crews, brands or event organisers?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
Not applicable (PASS). These would change the vote:
- **Network effect proven.** A small number of real crews (the founder sets the target number) run weekly crew runs for 8 or more weeks, with retention measured and reported.
- **Distribution with a cost.** One repeatable channel to crew leaders, with a measured cost per crew.
- **A defensible core.** Name the one crew mechanic a competitor cannot easily copy, and show that it works with real users. Phone verification must be live so the headcount bonus actually applies.
- **A revenue hypothesis.** Pick one, tested with at least one paying party.
- **Legal clearance** for points, trading and the location-service filing before launch.
